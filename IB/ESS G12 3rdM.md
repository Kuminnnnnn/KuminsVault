In this presentation, I will walk you through Switzerland’s energy consumption choice and trends.  
Before we begin, it’s important to note that Switzerland is a small, landlocked country in the heart of Europe, known for its mountainous terrain, limited natural fossil resources, and a long-standing commitment to environmental protection. These characteristics strongly influence how the country produces and consumes energy.
___
Now let’s look more closely at Switzerland’s 2024 energy mix, shown in the pie chart.

One of the most important observations is that **hydropower takes up of 33.2% and oil takes up of 32.5%. Together form the two pillars of Switzerland’s primary energy consumption**.  
Together, they make up almost **two-thirds** of the entire energy mix.

Hydropower represents Switzerland’s **clean, domestic, and renewable** energy foundation.  
In contrast, oil is a **high-carbon** fuel that Switzerland must **almost entirely import**.  
This creates a striking contrast within the same energy structure: **a very clean electricity system on one side, and a very carbon-intensive transport and heating system on the other**.

If we look at the low-carbon portion of the mix, hydropower (33.2%), nuclear energy (17%), and solar power (4.4%) together account for about **55%** of Switzerland’s primary energy consumption.  
And this number would actually be even higher if we only considered **electricity generation**, because oil is almost never used in power production.  
This means Switzerland’s **electricity system is already extremely low-carbon**, but its **end-use sectors—especially transport—are still dominated by fossil fuels**.

Natural gas appears with a moderate share at **10.4%**, used mainly for heating and industrial processes.  
Compared with countries like Germany or Italy, which rely more heavily on gas, Switzerland’s dependence on natural gas is relatively mild.

Finally, we see that **wind, biofuels, and other renewables remain very small**, together contributing less than **3%** to the total energy mix.  
This shows that when Switzerland talks about “renewable energy,” the vast majority still refers to **hydropower**, while wind and biomass serve only as minor supplements in the overall system.
___
Looking at the long-term trend, Switzerland’s total primary energy consumption grew steadily from **1965 to 2000**, driven by economic development and rising living standards.  
After 2000, total energy use leveled off or slightly declined.  
___
Oil consumption increased sharply between the 1960s and early 1970s, reaching a peak of **175 TWh in 1973**.  
However, after the oil crisis, and especially in the last two decades, oil use declined significantly.
___
Hydropower has remained **consistently high and stable** since the 1970s.  
Switzerland developed most of its hydropower potential early, so hydropower is no longer a growth driver—but it **continues to serve as the backbone** of the Swiss electricity system.
