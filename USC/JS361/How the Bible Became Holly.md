# Chapters 1–2 Study Notes

## From the Northern Kingdom to the Writings of Judah

## Central Argument

Satlow explains how ancient Israelite writings gradually moved from being practical or scholarly materials to possessing religious authority. This was not a sudden creation of “the Bible.” Instead, it was a long historical process shaped by political collapse, migration, scribal activity, royal power, and changing ideas about writing.

The overall development was:

**oral authority → scribal texts → written oracles → limited religious authority → attempted normative authority**

By 586 BCE, Judah had developed a significant literary culture, but there was still no complete Bible and no general expectation that everyone must obey written scripture.

## Chapter 1: The Northern Kingdom of Israel, 922–722 BCE

### 1. The Bible presents Israel through a Judahite perspective

The northern kingdom of Israel fell to Assyria in 722 BCE. The later Judahite author of Kings interpreted this disaster as divine punishment: Israel had sinned and refused to obey YHWH.

Satlow warns that this account is not politically neutral. Judah had long feared and envied its richer and more powerful northern neighbor. After Israel’s destruction, Judahite writers portrayed the northern kingdom as a moral failure whose fate demonstrated the consequences of disobedience.

Therefore, biblical history preserves genuine information, but it also reshapes the past according to Judahite religious and political interests.

### 2. Israel, rather than Judah, was the early regional power

During the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, Israel was wealthier, more populous, and more culturally developed than Judah. Samaria contained a sophisticated palace and expensive ivory objects, while Jerusalem remained a relatively small and poor mountain settlement.

Israel was not a modern centralized nation-state. It was a fragile confederation containing different ethnic, regional, and cultural groups. The monarchy controlled strategic centers such as Dan, Bethel, Hazor, and Megiddo, but military power alone could not hold the kingdom together.

### 3. Shared stories created an Israelite identity

Israel’s diverse population developed common traditions that allowed its members to imagine themselves as one people. The story of Jacob and his twelve sons provided a model for the tribal system:

- Jacob’s second name, Israel, connected the tribes to a shared ancestor.
- His sons represented the different tribes.
- The story acknowledged conflict among the tribes while presenting them as one family.
- Ephraim’s privileged position reflected its political leadership in the northern kingdom.

Satlow writes that the myth of the children of Israel “created a collective imagined community.” The story was probably not an accurate record of common biological descent. Its importance was social and political: it gave different groups a shared identity.

These traditions first circulated orally. Professional storytellers repeatedly performed and modified them, so early Israelite history was fluid rather than fixed in an authoritative written form.

### 4. Language and religion also unified the kingdom

The population gradually developed mutually understandable forms of Hebrew. The Samaria ostraca, administrative notes written on pottery fragments, may preserve a distinct northern dialect sometimes called “Israelian Hebrew.”

Religion provided another source of unity. YHWH became the national protector of Israel and was increasingly identified with the Canaanite high god El.

However, early Israelite religion was not yet strict monotheism. Satlow emphasizes:

> “YHWH was the ‘official’ god of Israel but not always the only one.”

People could publicly worship YHWH while privately honoring other deities. Inscriptions mention YHWH alongside El and Baal, and some Israelites may have understood Asherah as YHWH’s consort. Household figurines also show that private religious practices continued.

Shared observances—including sacrifice, festivals, Sabbath practices, circumcision, and avoidance of pork—helped distinguish Israelites from surrounding peoples.

### 5. Priests and prophets exercised oral authority

Priests maintained the relationship between the community and YHWH through sacrifices at temples and local shrines. Prophets communicated divine messages.

There were two general kinds of prophets:

- Institutional prophets worked within temples or royal courts and could be pressured to support the ruler.
- Independent prophets, such as Elijah, Elisha, Amos, and Hosea, could criticize the religious and political establishment.

Amos attacked people who observed rituals while exploiting the poor. This tension between correct ritual and ethical behavior later became a major theme of biblical prophecy.

### 6. Scribes were administrative and diplomatic elites

Scribes were more than copyists. They served as administrators, diplomats, translators, legal advisers, teachers, and intellectuals.

Because Israel and Judah were Assyrian vassal states, scribes had to manage international correspondence. They needed knowledge of local Hebrew, Aramaic, Akkadian, diplomatic conventions, and cuneiform writing.

Scribes belonged to a wider Near Eastern professional network. Their education included copying and revising older literary and legal compositions. They were expected to interact creatively with texts, not reproduce them mechanically.

### 7. Written law was not yet binding law

Legal disputes were normally resolved by elders and local leaders through negotiation. They did not usually consult an authoritative written code and mechanically apply its rules.

Satlow therefore argues:

> “Even the legal texts had more literary than normative authority at this time.”

The Laws of Hammurabi, for example, functioned partly as scribal training and partly as legal guidance. They were not equivalent to a modern statutory code.

### 8. The Covenant Code was probably a scribal exercise

Exodus 21–23 contains a collection now called the Covenant Code. Its language suggests a northern Israelite origin.

Satlow argues that it was primarily:

- a training text for scribes;
- a collection of model legal problems;
- a source of general guidance;
- an intellectual vision of justice.

Its rules were not necessarily binding. Their occasional impracticality makes sense if they were educational or idealistic exercises rather than enforceable statutes.

Satlow states directly:

> “The Covenant Code was a scribal exercise.”

An administrator might remember or consult it while resolving a dispute, but would not cite it as the final legal authority.

### 9. Scribes recorded selected prophetic oracles

Prophets normally delivered their messages orally, but scribes sometimes recorded them. The Deir Alla inscription about the prophet Balaam provides nonbiblical evidence for this practice.

Written prophecies were preserved selectively:

- failed predictions tended to disappear;
- apparently fulfilled predictions gained credibility;
- Amos and Hosea survived partly because Israel’s destruction seemed to confirm their warnings;
- their criticism of Israel also suited later Judahite attitudes toward the north.

Thus, preservation was not neutral. Historical outcomes and later political interests shaped which prophetic voices survived.

### 10. Assyria destroyed Israel and displaced its population

King Hoshea rebelled against Assyria and sought Egyptian assistance, but Egypt failed to rescue him. Assyrian campaigns devastated Israel, and Samaria fell in 722 BCE.

The Assyrians deported many Israelites and resettled other conquered peoples in the region. Some deportees entered the Assyrian military or administration, while others probably became agricultural laborers.

Many Israelites also fled south into Judah. Archaeological evidence shows rapid growth in Judahite settlements and a dramatic expansion of Jerusalem.

The refugees carried scrolls, but their most important contribution was their professional knowledge. As Satlow puts it:

> “More important than the actual texts that its refugees would bring to Judah were their skills, technologies, and ideas.”

At this stage, the refugees probably viewed the scrolls as tools of their occupation, not as sacred national treasures.

## Chapter 2: The Writings of Judah, 722–586 BCE

### 1. Israelite refugees transformed Judah

The refugees were probably not warmly welcomed, but Judah needed their expertise. Engineers, architects, administrators, and scribes entered the service of the Judahite state.

Over time, Judah even began identifying itself as “Israel,” showing the cultural influence of the northern immigrants.

Between 722 and 586 BCE, Judah developed a literary culture in which literacy and textual interpretation conferred social status. Scribes became more important, and the texts associated with them gained prestige.

### 2. Hezekiah used northern expertise

King Hezekiah appears to have accepted northern specialists into his administration. Their knowledge may have contributed to Jerusalem’s water system, especially Hezekiah’s Tunnel, and to the city’s defensive walls.

The Siloam Inscription commemorates the engineers’ achievement. Its existence also suggests the development of professional or craft literacy outside the traditional scribal bureaucracy.

Hezekiah’s multilingual officials further demonstrate that Judah now possessed scribes capable of diplomacy and international administration.

### 3. Jerusalem’s survival strengthened belief in YHWH

In 701 BCE, Sennacherib destroyed much of Judah, including Lachish, but failed to capture Jerusalem. Although Hezekiah paid a heavy tribute, the city’s survival was interpreted as miraculous proof that YHWH protected Jerusalem and Judah.

Isaiah had predicted that the city would survive. Because his central prediction appeared to come true, his other oracles also gained credibility and were preserved.

This event helped connect three ideas:

- YHWH had chosen Judah;
- some prophetic words accurately recorded divine will;
- written versions of those words deserved special respect.

### 4. Hezekiah attempted religious centralization

Hezekiah tried to suppress local shrines and concentrate public worship in the Jerusalem Temple. He promoted YHWH as Israel’s sole national god.

The reform did not last. His son Manasseh restored traditional shrines, sacred poles, and other established practices. Manasseh’s long and peaceful reign complicates the later biblical claim that these practices necessarily caused divine punishment.

### 5. Judah experienced a growth of literary activity

Administrative inscriptions became more common, and royal or aristocratic patrons supported scholarly scribal work. Satlow observes that “there was an explosion of literary activity in Judah.”

The Book of Proverbs reflects this environment. Its teachings were primarily addressed to educated elites who had to choose between wealth, royal service, and the pursuit of wisdom.

Proverbs 25:1 attributes one collection to “the officials of King Hezekiah,” suggesting that Hezekiah’s scribes copied and revised earlier material. The traditional attribution to Solomon probably added antiquity and prestige rather than identifying the actual author.

### 6. A Temple library probably preserved texts

Direct evidence is limited, but Satlow considers it likely that the Jerusalem Temple maintained a collection of documents. Concentrations of bullae—clay seals used to secure documents—support the existence of an archive.

Such a library would have been managed by priests and scribes. It was not a public library; it served a small literate elite as an administrative and intellectual center.

### 7. The core of Deuteronomy reworked the Covenant Code

Northern scribes brought the Covenant Code to Judah. During copying and study, scribes revised it. Satlow identifies one such revision with the central laws of Deuteronomy 12–26.

> “The core of Deuteronomy reworks the Covenant Code.”

The revisions follow a consistent program:

- political authority becomes more centralized;
- religious worship is restricted to the Jerusalem Temple;
- local festivals are brought under priestly supervision;
- sacrifice is explained less as food for YHWH and more as gratitude;
- the king himself is placed under written law.

Deuteronomy’s version of Passover, for example, combines previously local celebrations into one centralized festival in Jerusalem.

### 8. Deuteronomy may represent a “scribal fantasy”

Deuteronomy supports centralization, but it also limits royal power. The king must copy, read, and obey the law and may not accumulate excessive wealth or wives.

Because ancient Near Eastern kings rarely commissioned works subordinating themselves to law, Satlow doubts that Hezekiah ordered its composition. He instead proposes that scribes close to Hezekiah’s court produced it as an intellectual exercise—a vision of how society should operate if scribal principles governed it.

The scroll may then have been stored and forgotten in the Temple.

### 9. Josiah gave a written scroll official authority

In 622 BCE, a “book of Torah” was reportedly discovered during Temple repairs. Satlow identifies it with the core of Deuteronomy, although this remains a historical reconstruction rather than a certainty.

The scroll acquired authority through several human institutions:

- a priest discovered it;
- a scribe read and interpreted it;
- the prophetess Huldah authenticated its divine message;
- King Josiah publicly proclaimed it;
- the people participated in a covenant ceremony.

For a brief moment, Satlow writes:

> “A written document was given official religious authority.”

This was a major shift. A text was no longer merely a record or reference work; it was presented as a rule that the political community should obey.

Yet the scroll’s authority came from people and institutions:

> “Scrolls themselves never intrinsically possess authority.”

Josiah used the document to justify suppressing local shrines, eliminating traditional cults, centralizing Passover, and attacking rival priests.

### 10. Josiah’s reform was probably limited

Kings describes Josiah’s reform as comprehensive, but archaeological evidence does not show a complete transformation of Judahite religion. Household figurines continued to be used, local practices survived, and Josiah’s successors reversed his policies.

His campaign may therefore have been more successful rhetorically than socially. A newly authoritative text could not easily eliminate centuries of traditional practice.

### 11. The Deuteronomistic History reshaped the past

Scribes associated with Josiah expanded Deuteronomy and composed or edited the historical sequence from Joshua through Kings. Scholars call them the Deuteronomistic Historian or historians.

Their narrative advances two main claims:

1. Israel’s success or failure depends on obedience to YHWH.
2. Israel’s history reaches its ideal climax in Josiah’s reform.

The work used older legends and royal records but reorganized them according to a theological agenda. It was probably written mainly for rulers and court officials, teaching that the king’s religious behavior determined the fate of the nation.

When Josiah died in 609 BCE and Judah later collapsed, scribes revised the history. They explained the disaster as punishment for earlier rulers, especially Manasseh, rather than evidence against Josiah’s program.

### 12. Writing spread into prophecy, priesthood, and ritual

Judahite scribes collected and edited prophetic traditions associated with Isaiah, Jeremiah, Habakkuk, and others. Jeremiah’s scribe Baruch illustrates direct cooperation between prophet and scribe.

Priestly rituals were also written down. Materials now associated with the P source recorded sacrificial procedures, while later H or Holiness materials combined ritual instruction with ethical and social concerns.

Written blessings and amulets show that writing itself could possess numinous power. The silver scrolls from around 600 BCE contain language similar to the priestly blessing in Numbers 6:24–26. These words probably circulated independently before being incorporated into a larger biblical text.

### 13. Judah developed a culture of literacy, but not yet a scriptural religion

By the late seventh century BCE, literacy had become a status marker. The Lachish letters show that a military official considered an accusation of illiteracy insulting.

Nevertheless, textual authority remained restricted. Satlow concludes that only writings understood as accurate records of YHWH’s words possessed significant authority, and even those were not generally treated as binding commands.

Josiah’s attempt to make one scroll normatively binding was exceptional and largely unsuccessful.

|701 BCE|Jerusalem survives Sennacherib’s invasion; belief in YHWH’s protection and prophetic authority grows|
