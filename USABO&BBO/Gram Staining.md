Gram staining is a classic differential staining technique used to classify bacteria into ​**Gram-positive (G⁺, purple)** or ​**Gram-negative (G⁻, pink/red)** groups. Here’s a step-by-step breakdown of the procedure:

---

### ​**Procedure and Principles**

#### ​**1. Smear Preparation**

- ​**Steps**:
    - Spread a small bacterial sample evenly on a glass slide.
    - Heat-fix by passing the slide through a flame 2-3 times.
- ​**Purpose**: Kills bacteria and adheres the sample to the slide to prevent washing off.

#### ​**2. Primary Staining**

- ​**Reagent**: ​**Crystal Violet**.
- ​**Steps**: Flood the smear with crystal violet for ​**1 minute** → Rinse with water.
- ​**Principle**: Crystal violet penetrates the cell wall, staining all bacteria purple (both G⁺ and G⁻).

#### ​**3. Mordant Application**

- ​**Reagent**: ​**Lugol’s Iodine**.
- ​**Steps**: Apply iodine solution for ​**1 minute** → Rinse with water.
- ​**Principle**: Iodine binds crystal violet, forming an insoluble ​**crystal violet-iodine complex** to fix the stain.

#### ​**4. Decolorization**

- ​**Reagent**: ​**Ethanol/Acetone mixture (95% ethanol : acetone = 3:1)**.
- ​**Steps**: Add decolorizer dropwise until runoff is clear (~**10-30 seconds**, timing is critical) → Rinse immediately.
- ​**Key Mechanism**:
    - ​**G⁺ bacteria**: Thick peptidoglycan layer retains the complex, staying purple.
    - ​**G⁻ bacteria**: Thin peptidoglycan and outer membrane allow the complex to wash out, losing purple color.

#### ​**5. Counterstaining**

- ​**Reagent**: ​**Safranin or Basic Fuchsin**.
- ​**Steps**: Apply counterstain for ​**1 minute** → Rinse → Blot dry.
- ​**Principle**: Decolorized G⁻ bacteria take up the red counterstain; G⁺ bacteria remain purple.

#### ​**6. Microscopy**

- Observe under oil immersion: ​**Purple = G⁺, Pink/Red = G⁻**.
